\begin{abstract}
\noindent
The EU AI Act requires providers of high-risk systems to file technical
documentation describing how the system reaches its decisions. Annex~IV names no
method. We set out a reading of it under which circuit-level interpretability
evidence is the relevant evidence, mark that reading explicitly as an
interpretive choice, and then ask whether such evidence can support a conformity
claim.

We give a criterion. Evidence is \emph{filable at tolerance} $\alpha$ if the
modal claim commands at least $1 - \alpha$ of the space of defensible analytic
specifications. We then measure it. Across 15{,}840 pre-registered
specifications on GPT-2 small and the indirect object identification task, with
every axis level drawn from a published implementation, the derived Annex~IV
statement flips across 73.2\% of specification pairs (95\% CI 0.725 to 0.738)
and the modal claim commands 41.1\% of the space. The evidence is not filable at
any tolerance a conformity assessment body would plausibly accept.

Standardising the evaluation metric, the largest single lever, leaves 59.4\%.
Removing circuit size from the claim and holding it fixed leaves 27.1\%. The
underlying circuits are near-disjoint, at a median pairwise Jaccard overlap of
4\%, and functionally uncorrelated, at a Cohen's kappa of 0.015. The instability
is therefore not one mechanism described in different words.

Annex~IV point 4 already requires providers to justify the appropriateness of
their performance metrics. Our results make that requirement load-bearing rather
than formal. The study covers one model and one task.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

An audit means two auditors reach the same conclusion. That is not a definition
borrowed from machine learning. It is what makes an audit worth commissioning.

The EU AI Act \citep{aiact2024} requires providers of high-risk AI systems to
file technical documentation describing how their systems reach decisions, and
it gives a person subject to certain automated decisions a right to an
explanation of the main elements of the decision taken. Mechanistic interpretability is the obvious
source of that technical content, and circuit discovery is its most developed
instrument. A circuit is a subgraph of a model said to be responsible for a
behaviour. If a provider files a circuit-level account of why its system behaved
as it did, that filing is worth something only if a second analyst, given the
same system and the same tool, would file something compatible.

There is reason to doubt it before any experiment. Circuit discovery requires a
series of choices that the literature does not settle: which attribution
objective to optimise, which ablation operator to use, which corrupted inputs to
compare against, which faithfulness metric to score with, and where to cut the
ranking. Each choice has published defenders. None is currently reported as part
of a filing. A claim that changes with an unreported choice is not a property of
the system alone.

The pieces of this argument exist separately. Prior work shows that mechanistic
explanations need not be unique, that faithfulness scores move with ablation
methodology, and that different research groups have reached conflicting
conclusions about the same behaviour. What has not been done is to connect those
results to the filing: to ask how far a \emph{regulatory statement} moves across
the space of defensible choices, and to attach a decision rule that a standards
body could apply without adjudicating which choice is correct.

We do that. We state a reading of Annex~IV under which circuit-level evidence is
the relevant evidence, and we mark the reading as an interpretive choice rather
than a legal conclusion. We define a criterion, filability, that a conformity
procedure can apply. We then measure it over a pre-registered grid of 15{,}840
specifications, with every level of every axis taken from a published
implementation, and map each discovered circuit through deterministic code to a
structured Annex~IV statement. The statement flips across 73.2\% of
specification pairs. No single analytic choice rescues it, and the circuits
behind the conflicting statements are neither structurally nor functionally
interchangeable. The study covers one model and one task, which is the
limitation most likely to change the number.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item A reading of Annex~IV, stated as an explicit interpretive choice, under
which circuit-level evidence is the evidence the documentation duty calls for,
and an argument from the text for why the causal form of the question selects
it (section on Interpreting Annex~IV).
\item \emph{Filability}, a criterion a conformity procedure can apply without
deciding which analytic choice is correct, together with a five-step protocol
for applying it (section on The Filability Protocol).
\item A measurement of that criterion over a pre-registered grid, with a
deterministic map from circuit to regulatory statement (Results).
\item Evidence that the instability survives every available control:
standardising any single axis, removing circuit size from the claim, holding
size fixed, and comparison against a size-matched random null (Results,
Baselines).
\item A methodological result for multiverse designs generally. When
specifications select objects of different sizes, pooled statistics can invert,
and we report three of ours that do (Limitations).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Non-identifiability.}
\citet{meloux2025} ask whether a unique mechanistic explanation exists for a
given behaviour, judged by the field's own criteria. They answer that it need
not. That result is our premise. Our contribution is downstream of it. We
measure what non-identifiability costs when an explanation is filed with a
regulator rather than published in a paper.

\paragraph{Faithfulness metric sensitivity.}
\citet{miller2024} survey design choices in ablation-based faithfulness
measurement. They find that existing methods are highly sensitive to changes
that appear insignificant. They conclude that a faithfulness score reflects the
researcher's methodological choices as much as it reflects the circuit. They
establish that one axis matters. We cross seven, and we carry the result through
to a regulatory statement. We also use their released library as our instrument,
so this study is a stress test of the tooling by the tooling's own definitions.

\paragraph{Structure versus function.}
\citet{bayatmakou2026} vary input statistics while holding the task fixed. They
find that structurally distinct circuits can implement the same computation, and
they call this phantom specialisation. This is the strongest objection to our
design. If circuit discovery samples from an equivalence class of behaviourally
identical subgraphs, then a flip rate over claims measures only which member was
sampled. We answer the objection with a measurement rather than an argument, in
the section on structure versus function below. Our circuits are not
functionally interchangeable.

\paragraph{Variance as estimation.}
\citet{meloux2025b} treat mechanistic interpretability as statistical estimation
and analyse the variance of the estimator. Seed variance is one axis of our
specification space. Their framing is the statistical companion to what we do at
the level of the whole specification.

\paragraph{Auditability.}
\citet{lan2026} observe that two papers reached conflicting conclusions about the
same behaviour, and that a third found both partly correct and incomparable. They
call for auditing standards so that findings can be certified in safety-critical
settings. We answer that call with a measurement and a decision rule rather than
a proposal. \citet{sharkey2025} list open problems in the field, including the
socio-technical problems this paper touches. \citet{mueller2025} argue that
comparison across methods requires standardised evaluation. That argument is
adjacent to ours: certification requires standardised specification.

\paragraph{Conformity assessment and audit access.} Two governance literatures
meet at this paper and have not been connected. \citet{mokander2022} read the
Act as an attempt to build a Europe-wide ecosystem for AI auditing. Their
closing recommendation is that its vague concepts be translated into verifiable
criteria. Filability is one such translation: one provision, one threshold, one
measured value. \citet{casper2024} argue that black-box access is insufficient
for a rigorous audit, and that access to weights and activations lets an auditor
interpret a model more thoroughly. We take that conclusion as given and ask what
follows from it. White-box access establishes that a circuit can be recovered.
It does not establish that two auditors recover the same one. Neither work
measures the stability of the evidence an audit would rest on.

\paragraph{Explanation metrics under optimisation pressure.}
\citet{hsia2023} show that sufficiency and comprehensiveness can be inflated
without changing either the prediction or the explanation. Two of our four
evaluation metrics are those two. This is one reason we report the discard rate
for each metric separately rather than pooling the metrics silently.

\paragraph{Multiverse analysis.}
The design follows \citet{steegen2016}, who propose reporting results across the
space of defensible data-processing choices, and \citet{simonsohn2020}, whose
specification curve is the visualisation we adapt. \citet{simmons2011} named the
underlying problem: undisclosed flexibility in analysis lets an analyst present
almost anything as significant. We apply a methods literature from psychology to
an evidentiary question in machine learning. The transfer is not decorative. A
claim that an explanation is stable is exactly the kind of claim that undisclosed
flexibility can manufacture.

\section{Interpreting Annex IV}

Annex~IV requires technical documentation for a high-risk AI system. Nothing in
it names circuit discovery, mechanistic interpretability, or any other method.
This section sets out how we read the requirement and why. The reading is a
choice, and we mark it as one.

\paragraph{The broad reading.}
Annex~IV can be read broadly. Point 2(c) asks for a description of the system
architecture, explaining how software components build on or feed into each
other. Point 2(d) asks for the training data, its provenance and its
characteristics. Point 3 asks for monitoring, functioning and control. On a broad
reading, a provider documents what enters the system, what leaves it, and how the
parts are wired together. The duty is then discharged at the level of the system
rather than the model. A multi-agent deployment would be documented as a topology
of components. Nothing in that reading requires anyone to look inside a network.

We do not claim the broad reading is wrong. It is available, and for many
high-risk systems it may be sufficient.

\paragraph{The narrow reading we adopt.}
Two provisions ask for something the broad reading does not supply. Point 2(e)
requires an assessment of the technical measures needed ``to facilitate the
interpretation of the outputs of AI systems by the deployers''. Point 3 requires
the technical measures ``put in place to facilitate the interpretation of the
outputs''. The same concept appears twice, in two tenses: the measures needed,
and the measures taken.

Both provisions are about interpreting outputs. A deployer exercising human
oversight under Article 14 must be able to form a view about why a particular
output occurred. Article 86(1) sharpens this for the individual, who has a right
to ``clear and meaningful explanations of the role of the AI system in the
decision-making procedure and the main elements of the decision taken''.

We read ``the main elements of the decision'' as a causal claim, not a
correlational one. That is our interpretive choice, and the rest of this paper
depends on it.

\paragraph{Why the causal reading selects this evidence.}
% NOTE: deliberately argued from what each method DOES, not from a contested
% empirical claim about attention weights. If we later want to cite the
% attention-is-not-explanation literature, those references must be added to
% refs.bib and verified first. Do not add an unverified citation here.
Three families of evidence could be offered against these provisions, and they
differ in what they establish.

Black-box evaluation, including benchmarking and red-teaming, characterises what
a system does across a distribution of inputs. It establishes behaviour. It does
not identify which parts of the system produced a particular output, so it cannot
speak to the main elements of a specific decision.

Post-hoc attribution that reads off internal quantities, such as attention
weights or gradient-based saliency, reports an association between an internal
value and an output. Association is evidence about what covaries with the
decision. It is not evidence about what produced it.

Ablation-based circuit analysis operates differently. It removes a component and
measures the change in the output. That is an intervention on the system, and the
quantity it returns is a causal one. Of the three families, it is the one whose
operation matches the form of the question that Article 86(1) asks.

This does not make circuit analysis correct, complete, or the only admissible
evidence. It makes it the family whose output is the right \emph{kind} of object
for the narrow reading. That is the claim, and it is the only claim we need.

\paragraph{The metric provision, and why it is load-bearing.}
Point 4 requires ``a description of the appropriateness of the performance
metrics for the specific AI system''. Read quickly, alongside eight other
documentation duties, it looks like a formality.

Our results make it load-bearing. Standardising the evaluation metric removes
more claim instability than standardising any other axis in the space
(Table~\ref{tab:fixed}). The metric is therefore not a presentational detail of
the filing. It is a determinant of what the filing says. A provider who cannot
justify the choice of metric has not justified the claim that rests on it, and
\citet{hsia2023} show that the two metrics most used in this literature can be
inflated without changing either the prediction or the explanation.

\paragraph{What we are not claiming.}
We are not lawyers, and we do not claim to settle which reading of Annex~IV is
correct. We claim three things. The narrow reading is defensible on the text.
Under that reading, circuit-level evidence is the relevant evidence. And under
that reading, the evidence must be stable enough that two competent analysts
filing the same system would file compatible statements.

The rest of this paper measures whether it is.

\section{Formalism and the Claim Map}

A \emph{specification} is a tuple of analytic choices
$s = (o, a, d, m, \tau, P, r, g)$. Here $o$ is the discovery objective, $a$ the
ablation operator, $d$ the corruption distribution, $m$ the evaluation metric,
$\tau$ the size threshold, $P$ the prompt variant, $r$ the seed and $g$ the
granularity.

Circuit discovery returns $C(s) \subseteq E$, a subset of the model's edge set.
For GPT-2 small under the factorised graph with separate query, key and value
inputs, $|E| = 32{,}491$. We confirm this figure twice. The instrument reports
it, and a closed-form count over the computation order reproduces it
independently. In that count, block $b$ attention receives from $1 + 13b$ sources
at three input slots for each of 12 heads. MLP $b$ receives from those same
sources plus its own block's heads. The output receives from all 157
non-terminal sources. Circuits touch components drawn from 144 attention heads
and 12 MLPs, so 156 in total.

\paragraph{Circuit instability.}
For two specifications,
\begin{equation}
D(s_i, s_j) = 1 - \frac{|C_i \cap C_j|}{|C_i \cup C_j|},
\end{equation}
and $\bar{J}$ is the mean Jaccard similarity over unordered pairs.

\paragraph{Claim instability.}
Let $\varphi$ map a circuit to a structured Annex~IV statement. The flip rate is
the probability that two distinct specifications yield different statements,
\begin{equation}
F = 1 - \frac{\sum_c n_c (n_c - 1)}{N(N-1)},
\label{eq:flip}
\end{equation}
where $n_c$ counts specifications yielding claim class $c$ and $N$ is the number
of specifications. Equation~\ref{eq:flip} is the unbiased Gini--Simpson form. It
gives the probability that two draws without replacement differ. It is
unit-tested against the brute-force pairwise definition over randomised cases.
The modal share is $\pi^{*} = \max_c n_c / N$.

We also use a within-group form. For a set of conditioning axes $G$,
\begin{equation}
F_{\text{within}}(G) = 1 - \frac{\sum_g \sum_c n_{g,c}(n_{g,c}-1)}{\sum_g n_g(n_g-1)},
\label{eq:within}
\end{equation}
which weights every pair equally rather than every group equally. It reduces
exactly to Equation~\ref{eq:flip} when $G$ is empty.

\paragraph{Filability.}
Evidence is \emph{filable at tolerance} $\alpha$ if and only if
$\pi^{*} \geq 1 - \alpha$.

% CORRECTED 21 Sep. The arXiv version asserts "F <= 1 - 1/k". That is the
% asymptotic bound and is strictly FALSE at finite N: the unbiased
% Gini-Simpson form attains 0.889006 at N = 7,561 and k = 9, slightly above
% 1 - 1/9 = 0.888889. The difference does not move 82.3%, but a paper arguing
% for rigour should not state a false inequality. Verified numerically over the
% most even split of N into k classes.
A reported flip rate must be read against the number of classes the map can
produce. As $N$ grows, $F$ approaches a maximum of $1 - 1/k$ for $k$ claim
classes; at finite $N$ the exact maximum is attained by the most even split of
$N$ across the $k$ classes. With $N = 7{,}561$ and the nine classes observed
here that maximum is 0.8890. The observed $F$ of 0.7316 is 82.3\% of it. $F$ is
therefore not near one by construction.

\paragraph{The map itself.}
$\varphi$ is the most attackable choice in this paper. It is therefore fixed in
the pre-registration, implemented as deterministic code, and released on
publication. It is not generated by a language model. Generating it that way
would add model variance on top of circuit variance, with no way to separate the
two.

There are two addressees, and they follow the regulation rather than
convenience. $\varphi_{\text{overseer}}$ is built on Annex~IV 2(e) and 3
together with Article 14(4)(c). It is keyed on the dominant layer band, then the
size class, then the ranked input segment. $\varphi_{\text{affected}}$ is built
on Article 86(1) and leads with the input segment, because that right is owed to
the person the decision is about. Three granularities are nested by
construction, so a claim at a finer granularity refines the coarser one rather
than contradicting it.

A reviewer will suspect that the size bins were chosen to produce the reported
flip rate. They were not chosen. They were returned by a committed calibration
rule applied to a measured node-count curve. Table~\ref{tab:bins} reports the
sensitivity.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Model and task.}
GPT-2 small on indirect object identification \citep{wang2022}. This choice is
not an apology. The non-identifiability and faithfulness literature that this
paper extends works at this scale, so comparability is the point. The edge count
at this scale is also what makes a size-matched random baseline meaningful.

\paragraph{Grid.}
The pre-registered grid crosses seven discovery objectives, seven ablation
operators, four corruption distributions, four evaluation metrics, three size
thresholds, two prompt variants and five seeds. That is 1{,}540 discovery cells
and 18{,}480 specifications. The seed selects which prompts are sampled into the
evaluation set, so this axis is evaluation-set variability and not
nondeterminism in discovery. Corruption is nested within ablation rather than
crossed with it, because two of the seven ablation operators never read the
corrupt distribution. Crossing them would emit duplicate specifications.

Every level of every axis is taken from a published implementation. None was
invented for this study. Discovery uses the edge attribution patching family
\citep{syed2023} and integrated edge gradients, as implemented in the library
released with \citet{miller2024}. The automated circuit discovery lineage is
\citet{conmy2023}. The four corruption constructions are taken from
\citet{wang2022}. The ablation operators are the full set exported by the
library. \citet{li2024} motivate the choice of ablation operator as an analytic
decision rather than a detail.

\paragraph{Pre-registration.}
The analysis plan was committed and tagged before the confirmatory sweep began.
Both abstracts were drafted in advance, so neither direction could be written up
as a surprise. Deviations are recorded in an append-only file, with dates, and
with a note on whether each was decided before or after the affected result was
seen. The plan, the code and the per-cell records are released on publication.

\paragraph{Infrastructure.} Every cell ran on a single NVIDIA L4 under Linux
6.6 and Python 3.12.13, with auto-circuit 1.0.1, transformer-lens 2.18.0, torch
2.11.0+cu128, numpy 1.26.4, scipy 1.16.3 and statsmodels 0.14.6. Each result
carries a manifest recording its configuration, its seeds and a hash of that
environment. All 1{,}540 manifests report one identical environment
fingerprint, so no number reported here depends on a library version that
changed part way through the sweep.

\paragraph{Statistics.}
No $p$-value is computed across specifications anywhere. Specifications are a
designed grid, not an independent sample \citep{steegen2016,simonsohn2020}.
Uncertainty comes from a nonparametric bootstrap that resamples specifications
and never pairs, at $B = 10{,}000$ from a fixed seed of 0. This matters because $F$ and $\bar{J}$ are
U-statistics over pairs, and pairs that share a specification are dependent.
Intervals quantify uncertainty given the grid. They license nothing beyond it.

\paragraph{Two exclusions, with criteria fixed before any circuit was seen.}
One discovery objective does not execute. All 220 of its cells raise a runtime
error, from a mean squared error computed against an integral target, inside the
library's own code. We verified by digest over the sorted failing cell
identifiers that the failure set is exactly that arm and contains nothing else.
We did not repair it. The library is the instrument under test and is reproduced
verbatim. The realised grid is six objectives, 1{,}320 cells and 15{,}840
specifications.

The metric-relative threshold rule admits no circuit for 8{,}279 of the 15{,}840
specifications. That is a discard rate of 52.3\%, and it is distributed unevenly
across metrics (Table~\ref{tab:discard}). The unevenness was disclosed before the
plan was locked, from a six-cell validation slice. The surviving pool is
therefore 52\% comprehensiveness against 25\% by design. We report that
composition rather than smoothing it.

\subsection{The Result Under Maximum Pressure}

Strip every trace of circuit size from the claim, so that the statement is the
dominant layer band alone. Then hold circuit size fixed using
Equation~\ref{eq:within}. The result is $F = 0.2706$ (95\% CI 0.2550 to 0.2859).
The interval lower bound is above the pre-registered threshold. Where in the
model the behaviour is attributed still flips on 27\% of pairs when nothing
about size can contribute.

\subsection{Baselines}

We compare against four baselines, and we state them together here because each
one answers a different question about whether $F$ is large.

\emph{First, the attainable maximum.} $F$ is bounded above by the value it takes
when the $N$ specifications are split as evenly as possible across the $k$ claim
classes, which approaches $1 - 1/k$ as $N$ grows. With $N = 7{,}561$ and nine
classes that maximum is 0.8890, so the observed 0.7316 is 82.3\% of the
attainable maximum rather than a number near one by construction.

\emph{Second, chance overlap between circuits.} At the median circuit size of
200 edges, the analytic random line $k/(2|E|-k)$ gives 0.0031, so observed
overlap is about 45 times chance. Beating chance at this scale is not evidence
of much: a 500-edge circuit has an expected random overlap of 0.008.

\emph{Third, a size-matched random-circuit null multiverse}, drawn from the full
32{,}491-edge population with $R = 1{,}000$ null multiverses. Pooled, the
discovered multiverse is \emph{less} stable than the null: 0.7316 against
0.6774, with disjoint intervals. H3 is therefore rejected, since the
distributions are separated.

We do not report the direction of that separation as a finding, because it is an
artefact of the size distribution. Table~\ref{tab:bysize} shows why. The null is
degenerate above 1{,}000 edges, where a uniform draw touches all 156 components
and can produce only one claim. With size held fixed the ordering reverses:
discovered 0.2746 against null 0.4230 at medium granularity, and 0.2706 against
0.3822 at coarse. Discovered circuits are more stable than random ones once the
comparison is fair. They are still not stable enough to file.

\emph{Fourth, chance-corrected functional agreement.} Raw agreement between
circuits is not interpretable on its own, because two circuits that are right
about the same fraction of examples will agree often without agreeing on the
same examples. Cohen's kappa corrects for that, and we report it in the next
subsection.

\subsection{Structure Versus Function}

Per-example functional agreement between circuits is 0.6028, so functional
instability is 0.3972. The gap to the claim flip rate is 0.3344 (95\% CI 0.3297
to 0.3394), against a pre-registered threshold of 0.10. Agreement is computed
from per-example correct or incorrect verdicts, using the measures of
\citet{bayatmakou2026}, reproduced verbatim.

Cohen's kappa is 0.0146. The 60\% raw agreement is almost entirely explained by
both circuits being right about the same fraction of examples, not the same
examples. Kappa stays between 0.016 and 0.047 at every circuit size, while raw
agreement climbs from 0.52 to 0.92 and accuracy climbs from 0.51 to 0.95.

This answers the phantom specialisation objection. If discovery sampled from an
equivalence class of behaviourally identical subgraphs, the circuits would be
functionally interchangeable. At a kappa of 0.015 they are not.

H4 is supported on the pre-registered quantity. The gap does not survive holding
size fixed: it fails the 0.10 threshold at 6 of 10 sizes and is negative at 5. At
both ends of the range the mechanisms differ more than the filings do, and the
pooled figure is carried by the 500 to 2{,}000 edge band alone. We report the
pooled result because it was pre-registered, and the decomposition beside it
because the pooled result alone would mislead.

\section{The Filability Protocol}

\begin{framed}
\noindent\textbf{Filability of an interpretability claim.} For a filed claim $c$
derived from a circuit:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item Declare the specification space $\mathcal{S}$: the analytic axes, and the
levels of each that a competent analyst could defend, each with a citation.
\item Declare the claim map $\varphi$ as deterministic, published code. Not a
language model.
\item Compute $\pi^{*} = \max_c n_c / N$ over $\mathcal{S}$, and $F$ by
Equation~\ref{eq:flip}.
\item The claim is filable at tolerance $\alpha$ if $\pi^{*} \geq 1 - \alpha$.
\item Report $\pi^{*}$, $F$, the discard rate per axis level, and the
within-group decomposition of any pooled statistic.
\end{enumerate}
\end{framed}

\noindent
A filing that cannot state its own $\pi^{*}$ has not been audited. It has been
asserted.

The protocol does not ask the regulator to adjudicate which analytic choice is
correct. That is the property that makes it implementable. It asks only that the
filer declare the space and report how much of that space agrees.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{What standardising one axis buys.}
Less than it appears. The evaluation metric is the biggest lever, and it removes
0.1377 of a 0.7316 flip rate. There is no single knob. A procedure that
standardised one axis and declared the problem solved would be mistaken by a
wide margin.

\paragraph{What this means for a conformity procedure.}
The protocol in the previous section does not ask a regulator to decide which
ablation operator is correct. That is the property that makes it implementable,
because a conformity assessment body has no basis on which to adjudicate an open
methodological question in machine learning, and should not be asked to.

It asks something a body can do. Require the filer to declare the space of
defensible choices, and to report how much of that space agrees. Annex~IV point
4 already requires the provider to justify the appropriateness of the
performance metrics. Our results turn that requirement from a formality into a
measurement: metric choice moves the filed claim more than any other analytic
decision, so a metric justification that does not report the claim's sensitivity
to it has justified nothing.

\paragraph{On the instrument.}
One of seven documented, publicly exported discovery objectives does not run on
the library's own canonical task. We report this as a discard with its rate, and
as an observation about the maturity of the tooling that regulatory evidence is
expected to rest on. It is not this paper's headline. It is also not a criticism
of a library whose authors are among those calling for the field to address
exactly this \citep{sharkey2025}.

\paragraph{What a regulator can take from this.}
Not that interpretability evidence is worthless. Rather, that its evidentiary
weight is a measurable property rather than an assumed one, and that the
measurement is cheap relative to the sweep that produced the circuit in the
first place.

\section{Limitations}

\paragraph{One model, one task.}
Larger models have more components, so both the layer band and the size class
have more room. The direction of the effect on $F$ is not obvious. This is the
limitation most likely to change the number.

A second-model replication is pre-registered but not yet run: a 924-cell grid on
Pythia-160m, a model of a different architecture family that performs the same
task. It is identical on every axis except that the seed axis carries three
levels rather than five. The analysis plan, the reduced grid, and the criteria
for what would count as replication rather than refutation were committed and
timestamped before any cell of it ran. It will be reported whichever direction
it falls. A second task remains designed and not run.

\paragraph{Circuit size is an outcome, not an axis.}
It is chosen by the threshold rule, and it has distorted three separate pooled
quantities: $F$ itself, the direction of the null comparison, and the sign of
the functional gap. Fixing it drops $F$ from 0.7316 to 0.2746, a larger
reduction than standardising any pre-registered axis. We take this to be a
general hazard for multiverse designs whose specifications select objects of
different sizes. We therefore report every pooled statistic with its within-size
decomposition.

\paragraph{The multiverse is as wide as the library, not the literature.}
Optimal ablation \citep{li2024} is a defensible operator that the instrument
does not implement, so it never entered the space. The measured $F$ is therefore
a lower bound with respect to the field's full set of defensible choices.

\paragraph{The discard rate is high and uneven.}
It is 52.3\%, and the surviving pool is imbalanced across metrics. The threshold
rule was fixed in advance and no failing item was repaired. The composition is
nonetheless a property of the reported set, so we state it rather than adjust
for it.

% Added 21 Sep. Pre-empts the strongest remaining objection: that F is an
% artefact of which specifications survived the discard rule. The bound already
% exists in tab:fixed and was simply never connected to this limitation.
The effect of that imbalance on the headline figure is bounded by a result we
already report. Standardising the evaluation metric removes the imbalance by
construction, because each group then contains a single metric. It leaves
$F = 0.5939$ (Table~\ref{tab:fixed}). The instability therefore does not depend
on the metric composition of the surviving pool.

\paragraph{The bootstrap is invalid for one reported family.}
Where the conditioning groups hold only two to five specifications, retained
self-pairs dominate and the intervals are unusable. Those quantities are
reported as point estimates with the diagnostic attached. We did not construct a
bias-corrected estimator after seeing the naive intervals disagree, because
choosing an estimator in response to a result is the behaviour this paper
documents.

\section{Conclusions}

We asked whether circuit-level interpretability evidence survives the condition
under which it would be relied upon: two competent analysts, the same system,
the same tool, different defensible settings. On GPT-2 small and indirect object
identification, across 15{,}840 pre-registered specifications, it does not. The
derived Annex~IV statement flips across 73.2\% of specification pairs, the modal
claim commands 41.1\% of the space, and the evidence fails the filability
criterion at every tolerance a conformity assessment body would plausibly
accept.

The finding is not that discovered circuits are meaningless. Held at fixed size
they are more stable than a size-matched random null, 0.2746 against 0.4230. The
finding is that the filing derived from them is not determined by the system
under examination. It is determined jointly by that system and by analytic
choices that nobody is currently required to report.

The remedy we propose does not ask a regulator to settle which choice is
correct. It asks the filer to declare the space and to report how much of that
space agrees. A filing that cannot state its own $\pi^{*}$ has not been audited.
